Formula review: gamma and rho for MA(1) and AR(1)
Before simulating the five exercises, we need a short formula reference. This section is for reading the code
and interpreting its output, not for reproducing every probability derivation.
Two symbols that must not be confused
• 𝛾𝑘, read “gamma at lag k,” is the autocovariance at lag 𝑘. In particular, 𝛾0 is the variance.
4
• 𝜌𝑘, read “rho at lag k,” is the autocorrelation at lag 𝑘: 𝜌𝑘= 𝛾𝑘/𝛾0. It has no units and always lies
between -1 and 1.
Therefore, a value such as gamma0 = 2.7778 is not an ACF bar. It is a variance. A value such as rho1 =
0.8 is the theoretical height of the lag-1 ACF bar.
MA(1) reference
For the zero-mean MA(1) process
𝑋𝑡= 𝜖𝑡+ 𝜃𝜖𝑡−1,
Var(𝜖𝑡) = 𝜎2,
the useful results are:
𝐸(𝑋𝑡) = 0,
𝛾0 = (1 + 𝜃2)𝜎2,
𝛾1 = 𝜃𝜎2,
𝛾𝑘= 0 for 𝑘≥2,
𝜌0 = 1,
𝜌1 =
𝜃
1 + 𝜃2 ,
𝜌𝑘= 0 for 𝑘≥2.
An added constant changes only the mean. For example, 𝑍𝑡= 10 + 𝑋𝑡has mean 10 but exactly the same 𝛾𝑘
and 𝜌𝑘values as 𝑋𝑡. An MA(1) is stationary for every finite theta; its theoretical ACF cuts off after lag 1.
AR(1) reference
For the zero-mean AR(1) process
𝑌𝑡= 𝛼𝑌𝑡−1 + 𝑤𝑡,
Var(𝑤𝑡) = 𝜎2,
the process is stationary only when |𝛼| < 1. Under that condition:
𝐸(𝑌𝑡) = 0,
𝛾0 =
𝜎2
1 −𝛼2 ,
𝛾𝑘= 𝛼𝑘𝛾0,
𝜌𝑘= 𝛼𝑘
(𝑘≥0).
Thus an AR(1) theoretical ACF does not cut off: it decreases geometrically. A positive alpha creates positive
decay; a negative alpha makes the ACF alternate signs. The PACF, rather than the ACF, cuts off after lag
1.

𝑍𝑡= 10 + 𝜖𝑡−0.6𝜖𝑡−1.

Generate the MA(1) series: arima.sim()
arima.sim() generates a simulated ARMA time series. For the MA(1) call below, read its inputs from the
inside out:
• model = list(ma = -0.6) is a named list describing the model.
The name ma tells R to use a
moving-average term; -0.6 is its one coeﬀicient, so this is MA order 1. We do not write ma = "-0.6",
because the coeﬀicient must be a number, not text.
• n = 400 requests 400 output observations. A longer series makes the sample ACF less noisy than a
very short series would be.
• innov = innov_ma1 supplies the actual white-noise innovation vector 𝜖𝑡used by the simulation. innov
is short for innovations: the new random shocks entering the model at each time point.
If we omitted innov, arima.sim() could generate its own innovations. We create and name them ourselves
because then students can see exactly where the 𝜖𝑡’s in the mathematical model enter the R call. arima.sim()
7
returns a one-dimensional ts object containing the zero-mean MA part. We add 10 afterward so that the
simulated series matches 𝑍𝑡, whose model mean is 10.
# Keep the innovations as a separate object. It is the sequence epsilon_t in
# the model, with one fresh random shock at each simulated time point.

How to explain these six panels in a report:
1. Top left, random walk: each value contains the accumulated history of its earlier shocks. A local
decline is possible, but the positive drift = 0.3 makes an overall upward movement common in this
simulation. The increasingly remote values from the start should not be described as a stable horizontal
process.
2. Top middle, ACF before differencing: lag 0 is 1. The other bars remain very high and decline
slowly. Adjacent and moderately distant values look similar because they share much of the same
accumulated shock history.
3. Top right, PACF before differencing: it may show a very large lag-1 bar and other visible bars.
Do not call this an AR(1) from the PACF. A random walk is nonstationary, so AR order-identification
rules for stationary series do not apply to this raw plot.
4. Bottom left, first difference: diff() subtracts adjacent cumulative values, so it removes the old
accumulated history and leaves the new increment. The grey dashed line at 0.3 marks the theoretical
mean increment. The series should jump around this line rather than keep trending upward.
5. Bottom middle, ACF after differencing: aside from lag 0, most bars should lie near zero and
within the blue reference bands. This is graphical evidence that the differenced series behaves like
white noise around its nonzero mean of 0.3.
6. Bottom right, PACF after differencing: aside from chance sample bars, it should also have no
sustained pattern. ACF and PACF together support the same conclusion: after differencing, there is
no AR or MA structure left to identify in this simulated example.
One implementation detail is useful here: acf() and pacf() remove the sample mean by default. That is

why they can assess serial dependence in diff_y_random_walk even though the differenced values fluctuate
around 0.3 rather than around zero.
This process is called a random walk with drift. The drift is 0.3.
